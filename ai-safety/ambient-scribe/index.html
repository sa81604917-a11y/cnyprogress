Using an Ambient Scribe Well

An ambient scribe listens to the visit and drafts your note. DAX Copilot is the best known example. Others include NextGen Ambient Assist and Abridge. The steps on this page work for all of them.

The tool is fast. Your job is to make sure the note is true.

A 3 minute video walkthrough is in the works. The full guide is below.

The basic workflow

  1. Set up. Open the app and pick the right patient before you walk in.
  2. Ask, then record. Get consent first. Start the recording. Say the patient’s name and the reason for the visit out loud.
  3. Review. When the draft appears, read it against the transcript. The transcript is what was actually said.
  4. Edit, then sign. Fix what is wrong. Delete what you cannot confirm. Then sign.

Vendor guides with screenshots are linked at the end of this page. They cover the buttons. The rest of this page covers the judgment.

Tell it what you want

The default note is the vendor’s style, not yours. Change it:

  • Length. Style settings let you pick concise notes or fuller ones, and save that as your default. There is no word counter, but shorter is a setting, not a hope.
  • Section by section. A tight HPI in three or four sentences. A fuller assessment and plan. Your call.
  • Format. Bullets or prose, your headings, and what to leave out entirely.
  • Save it. Set your preferences once, in the style settings or a template, so every note starts the way you like it.

You are in control here. The scribe works in your style, not the other way around.

Ask first: a consent script

Use plain words. Here is a script that works:

“I use a tool that listens to our visit and drafts my note. That way I can focus on you instead of the computer. It does record our conversation. You can say no, and it will not change your care. Is that okay?”

If the patient hesitates, turn it off. Follow your own organization’s consent policy. Some settings require written consent.

Verify before you sign

Scribes write fluent notes. Fluency is not accuracy. Check these every time:

  • Negations. No, denies, never. A dropped “no” flips the meaning of a sentence.
  • Left and right. Laterality errors are common and easy to miss.
  • Doses and drug names. Check every number and every medication.
  • Dates and durations. “Three days” and “three weeks” are not the same history.
  • Invented findings. If the note says it and nobody said it, it comes out.
  • Who said what. The scribe tries to tell speakers apart, and often gets it wrong. It is weakest when family, an interpreter, or staff join the visit, and when people talk over each other. A symptom can end up credited to the wrong person. Check every attribution.

Simple rule: if a sentence matters and you cannot confirm it from the transcript, delete it.

When to keep it off

  • The patient says no, or seems unsure.
  • A third person is in the room and has not agreed to be recorded.
  • The conversation is sensitive enough that a recording would change what the patient tells you.
  • Any visit where you would not want a transcript to exist.

Notes for psychiatry and behavioral health

  • Risk statements must be exact. A summary can soften a threat, a plan, or a denial. Read those lines word for word.
  • Quotes must be real. If the note puts words in quotation marks, the patient must have said those words.
  • Collateral counts. Family members often join by phone or in the room. Keep their statements separate from the patient’s.
  • Substance use details matter. Amounts, routes, and last use are clinical facts, not color. Verify them like doses.
