Data Center Negotiation Toolkit

Updated October 2026

A practical toolkit for local officials negotiating data-center development from a position of strength: principles, model terms, U.S. deal comparisons, and the sources and reporting behind the comparisons. Written for New York; adaptable elsewhere.

Happening now

October 21, 2026: Indianapolis’s Metropolitan Development Commission votes on a $3.4 billion Metrobloks data center tax package — more than $183 million in projected tax savings for 35 permanent jobs. Read the case study: six things to fix before the vote.

Core principle: impact mitigation ≠ community benefit.

Developer costs (not benefits): grid upgrades, roads, water, wastewater, fire and EMS, noise mitigation, environmental remediation, decommissioning. These merely keep the public from losing money.

Community value (what the public gains): taxes and PILOT revenue, recurring host payments, community investment, housing, schools, child care, workforce investment. A strong deal requires both.

If you only read one page

I am a local official → I am reviewing a proposed deal → I am a resident or reporter →

Before considering incentives, require the developer to disclose: maximum contracted MW (megawatts); total tax exemptions and PILOT (payment in lieu of taxes) payments; who pays for grid, water, road, fire/EMS, and other project costs; permanent jobs and wages; community investment above impact mitigation; decommissioning security and financial guarantees.

New York’s pause and the state’s template

In July 2026, Executive Order 62 paused specified DEC discretionary approvals for data centers of 50 megawatts (MW) or more while the state completes its environmental and grid-policy work, including a Generic Environmental Impact Statement (GEIS) by the Department of Public Service. The administration has described the pause as lasting up to one year; permits issued by local governments are not covered. In September, Empire State Development released its voluntary Host Community Investment Framework, recommending a baseline of $1 million in community investment per MW of utility demand. The pause buys time. The state template is a floor, not a ceiling. Local officials can and should negotiate stronger terms than the voluntary framework requires. The Onondaga Gold Standard term sheet — a New York-specific adaptation of the 30-point model sheet, available as a Word download under Editable copies below — is written for that purpose.

The lesson of Micron: the Clay semiconductor megafab was negotiated largely behind closed doors, leaving the community little leverage over the final terms. Read the full sourced deal record, including action items for a community benefits agreement. Publish your negotiating terms before the next developer arrives, not after.

Reading the numbers

Three different measures get confused. Keep them separate:

  • Upfront or lifetime community investment — New York’s framework recommends at least $1 million per MW of utility demand. A flexible target negotiated locally, not a recurring payment and not necessarily cash to the host community.
  • Recurring annual tax or PILOT revenue — West Feliciana Parish projects roughly $273,000 per MW per year through a PILOT/lease structure. This is government revenue, not a payment on top of taxes.
  • Recurring community payments above taxes — a separate category, negotiated deal by deal.

Do not compare a one-time per-MW figure with an annual per-MW figure. They measure different things.

Responses to common objections

“Why not just ban data centers?”
A ban in one town often moves the project to the next town over, which then negotiates without your terms. The jobs, the tax base, and the leverage leave with it. It can go further than the next town: in Boulder City, Nevada, residents defeated a data center on private land in summer 2026, and the developer moved to adjacent federal land instead — beyond any town’s reach at all. Read the case study. A community that publishes strong terms before the developer arrives keeps the project and sets the price. West Feliciana Parish, Louisiana, did not ban its data center. Parish officials project roughly $273,000 per megawatt per year in PILOT/lease revenue — government revenue replacing conventional taxation, not an extra payment on top of taxes — which funds real services. A ban avoids the burdens, but it funds nothing.
“Tough terms will drive developers away.”
Predictability is what developers want. Clear published terms give them a predictable negotiating framework and reduce the risk that communities negotiate project by project without consistent benchmarks.
“Developers say tough terms mean delays and uncertainty.”
Grid-interconnection queues are genuinely long and uncertain, and developers are right to worry about timelines. That is an argument for published terms, not against them: a clear public framework that both sides can plan around reduces negotiation risk far more than project-by-project fights with shifting demands.
“This will hurt American competitiveness with China.”
A town negotiating water protections and ratepayer safeguards is not a national ban. The competition argument belongs in state and federal policy debates — it is not a reason for a county legislature to give away its tax base. Tough local rules do not stop the industry, either: Loudoun County, Virginia, the world’s largest data center hub, ended by-right zoning for data centers in 2025 and now requires board review of new projects. The buildout continues.
“Data centers bring jobs and investment.”
Some do. The question is never whether to welcome investment but on what terms. Costs caused by the project — grid upgrades, water strain, higher residential rates — are not community benefits. Get them in writing before the permits are issued.
