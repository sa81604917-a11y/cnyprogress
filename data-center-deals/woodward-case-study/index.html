Case study: Woodward–Microsoft

A 600-acre data center campus with unusually strong opening pledges — no tax incentives, developer-paid infrastructure — and nothing in writing yet. The leverage window is open now, before the development agreement is negotiated.

Published October 13, 2026. No development agreement has been negotiated; figures below are from reporting available at publication and may change.

What is known so far

Microsoft data center campus, Woodward, Iowa (Dallas County)
Site More than 600 acres near Highway 141 and Seminole Avenue; purchase closed about October 8, 2026 (price undisclosed; a 59-acre listing in the area advertised $62,400/acre)
Town Woodward, population roughly 1,300, about 30 miles northwest of Des Moines
Company pledges Microsoft says it will cover electrical infrastructure, road, and utility upgrades and will not seek tax incentives
Jobs At least 50 direct Microsoft workers per building (company figure); number of buildings and total jobs unknown
Timeline Two to three years for campus design, then about two years to complete the first building
Use Cloud computing and data storage; not a dedicated AI training center (per Microsoft)
Unknowns Water consumption (no design yet — “no idea what mechanical systems will be going into the buildings”), building count, total employment, noise, decommissioning
Status No development agreement negotiated yet; any agreement requires City Council approval. First public disclosure was an October 12, 2026 residents-only meeting.

What is genuinely good here

  • No tax incentives sought. The single most common way towns lose data-center deals — the abatement — is off the table by the company’s own statement. Compare Indianapolis, where the package under vote trades roughly $183 million in tax savings for 35 jobs.
  • Developer-paid infrastructure. Electrical, roads, and utilities covered by Microsoft means the town is not subsidizing the project’s own costs — the core of this toolkit’s “impact mitigation is not community benefit” principle.
  • Early disclosure of the no-incentive pledge. Stating it publicly before negotiations creates a commitment the town can hold the company to in writing.

What is missing or wrong

  • Nothing is in writing. Verbal pledges at a public meeting are not terms. Until the development agreement is negotiated and approved, the no-incentive pledge and the infrastructure commitment are promises, not obligations.
  • Water is undetermined. The largest open environmental question — how much water the campus will consume and from where — cannot be answered until the design exists. The agreement should require disclosure and caps before construction, not after.
  • The process has damaged trust. The October 12 unveiling was residents-only with IDs checked at the door; the opening questions were scripted; residents’ September questions about a nondisclosure agreement went unanswered in the minutes; and the June rezoning of the site passed with the usual additional readings waived. A town of 1,300 turned out 300 people — nearly one in four residents — and the meeting ended with a resident forcibly removed. Good terms negotiated through a process the town does not trust will still fail politically.
  • No public vote. When asked directly whether residents would get a vote, the mayor answered no. The development agreement — the one binding document — is therefore the only remaining venue for public leverage.

The leverage window: what to lock in now

  1. Put the pledges in the agreement. The no-tax-incentive commitment and the infrastructure obligations should be written into the development agreement as binding terms, not left as meeting statements.
  2. Require water disclosure and caps before construction. Projected consumption, source, and who pays for water infrastructure — disclosed and capped in the agreement, with monitoring.
  3. Consider the Ames model. Nearby Ames approved a nine-month pause and formed a resident study committee to examine data-center effects before deciding. Linn, Woodbury, and Marshall counties and the city of Nevada have all enacted moratoriums. A pause to study terms is not opposition; it is negotiation.
  4. Negotiate in public. The development agreement should be drafted with community input while terms are still movable — not presented after the fact. Publish the draft agreement before the council vote on it.
  5. Plan for decommissioning. A multi-building campus with a decades-long life needs end-of-life terms — financial assurance and site restoration — written now, not when the buildings are obsolete.

The contrast with Indianapolis is the point of pairing these two case studies: Indianapolis shows what happens when the terms are set before the community arrives; Woodward shows the moment before the terms are set, when the town’s leverage is at its maximum. The pledges are the best opening a host community could ask for. Writing them down is the work.
