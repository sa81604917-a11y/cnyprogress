A public resource for local and state officials

Who else is writing the data-center deal terms?

About 20 public-side publications were examined in depth for this page, with roughly six additional resources identified but not reviewed in full. This page maps what is out there, what each publication costs to access, the strongest public-side terms anyone has assembled, and a model package you can adapt, with each idea credited to its source.

Last updated: October 2026. Sources were checked live on October 1, 2026; the model package below was expanded that day in response.

Core principle

Costs caused by the project are not community benefits. Impact mitigation (grid, roads, water, wastewater, fire/EMS, noise, environmental cleanup, decommissioning) merely keeps the public from losing money and is non-negotiable. Community value (taxes/PILOT, recurring host payments, community investment, housing, schools, child care, workforce investment, residential energy relief) is what the public gains for the use of its scarce land, electricity, water, and infrastructure. A strong deal requires both.

01How to use this page

1.

See what already exists. Section 7 lists the public-side templates, guides, and ordinances with plain-language summaries, what each one covers, and what it costs to access. Most are free downloads your office can use today.

2.

Borrow the strongest terms. Sections 3 through 6 assemble a 30-term model package that draws on the best ideas found anywhere, with each addition credited to what it came from.

3.

Know the limits. Section 10 flags what could not be verified, and section 9 lists topics the whole landscape still skips.

This is a public resource published without an author name. It is not legal advice; treat it as a starting point, then have your municipal counsel confirm what fits your state's law.

02The landscape in brief

The public-side landscape is crowded but uneven. About twenty publications were examined in depth, nearly all of them free. But most are checklists or high-level principles. Only a few are actual model legal text or term sheets you could hand to your attorney and negotiate from.

  1. 1.

    Yes, many others publish data-center negotiation tools. Governments, nonprofits, journalists, academics, and a law firm have all published guides, checklists, and model documents for host communities. This page catalogs them (section 7) so you do not start from a blank page.

  2. 2.

    Depth varies widely. Only the NAACP's 2026 community-benefits template and the Kentucky Resources Council's model zoning ordinance are full model legal text. New York State's Community Investment Framework is the only government-issued template, though its template file itself could not be verified. Everything else runs from county checklists to state policy menus.

  3. 3.

    Almost everything is free. No template, guide, or model ordinance was found behind a paywall, a login, or an NDA gate. The one login-walled item is the National League of Cities' key-questions guide. The paid side is bespoke legal and consulting work, not documents for sale (section 8).

  4. 4.

    Our model package filled its gaps from this landscape. In October 2026 it added ten terms and two principles and strengthened six existing terms, borrowing from the NAACP, the New York State framework, the Kentucky Resources Council, WRI, and others. Section 4 shows which idea came from where.

  5. 5.

    Two topics are missing everywhere. No publication found covers property-tax assessment methodology for data centers, or electromagnetic fields (EMF) from the facilities (section 9).

03The model package: 10 principles and 30 terms

This is a host-community package you can adapt: a short framework brief built on 10 principles, plus a 30-term model term sheet grouped by topic. Ten new terms were added in October 2026: seven came from reviewing every publication on this page, and three more came from a follow-up expert review of the package's earlier form; the two new principles and the strengthened terms were added at the same time. New terms are labeled New; terms that were strengthened are labeled Extended. The peer review behind this page compared the package's earlier form (8 principles, 20 terms); every October 2026 addition closes a gap one of those reviews found, so all comparisons on this page apply to the current 10-principle, 30-term version.

Starting premise: most real data-center deals are negotiated under nondisclosure agreements, so communities negotiate without knowing what comparable towns have gotten. This package assumes communities know each deal's terms, compare them against public benchmarks, and never accept secrecy on the public side of the deal.

The 10 principles

  1. Developer pays for incremental grid infrastructure. The project's own grid buildout is its own cost, and the contract reserves grid-emergency curtailment rights. Extended

  2. Protect existing ratepayers from cost shifting. No household or business should pay more because a data center connected.

  3. Assign water and wastewater costs contractually. Who pays for extra water and sewer capacity goes in writing; treated potable water is off-limits above a large-user threshold. Extended

  4. Tie tax breaks to measurable benefits disclosed per megawatt. Any payments in lieu of taxes (PILOTs) or abatements scale to verified public value, not a flat deal.

  5. Index community-benefit payments to project scale, not flat fees. Payments rise with megawatts and adjust with inflation.

  6. Require public transparency and reporting on power, water and compliance. Reporting is developer-funded and independently reviewed, and includes a cumulative air-quality impact assessment. Extended

  7. Clawbacks for unmet commitments. Missed milestones cost the developer automatically, with minimum operating duration and exit fees so the community is not left holding stranded assets. Extended

  8. Negotiate from a common countywide framework. Towns are not played against each other; every community starts from the same floor.

  9. Require labor standards. Local hiring, prevailing wage, and apprenticeships are part of the deal. New

  10. Steer projects to appropriate sites. Brownfields and industrial land preferred over farmland and sensitive habitat. New

The 30 terms

Fiscal terms 1 to 4

  1. Full taxation as the default. Any tax break must be earned against verified public benefits, not granted up front.

  2. A contractual tax floor. The developer commits in writing to a minimum tax floor, so the deal cannot be eroded quietly later.

  3. Annual community payments indexed to project scale with inflation ratchets. Payments track megawatts of demand and rise with inflation rather than freezing at signing values. Any project expansion automatically reopens project economics: more community investment, higher annual payments, and fresh water, sewer, grid, decommissioning, and environmental review. The term also keeps the three money measures separate: upfront or lifetime community investment, recurring annual tax or PILOT revenue, and recurring community payments above taxes. Extended Oct 2026

  4. No vote without a 30-day public comparison against benchmarks. The deal sits in public view for 30 days next to comparable-deal benchmarks before any board vote.

Power 5 to 7

  1. Developer pays 100% of incremental grid infrastructure. Substations, lines, and upgrades the project requires are the project's bill.

  2. Explicit ratepayer protection. The agreement states directly that existing customers bear none of the project's costs.

  3. Minimum clean-energy share. A defined fraction of project power comes from clean energy, with milestones.

Water 8 to 10

  1. Water and wastewater costs assigned in writing. No handshake promises on who funds treatment and capacity.

  2. Closed-loop cooling caps and a drought hierarchy that puts homes first. Water-hungry cooling is capped, and treated potable water is barred above a large-user threshold. Water sources follow a hierarchy: reclaimed or non-potable water first, then closed-loop systems, then potable water only where necessary. All of it sits under a maximum daily and annual withdrawal, drought-stage restrictions, and a hard priority for residential and public-health supply, with all use metered and publicly reported. Extended Oct 2026

  3. Developer-funded water improvements deeded to the public. Upgrades the project pays for become public assets.

Neighbor protections 11 to 14

  1. Private-well guarantees. Neighboring well owners get monitoring and a guaranteed remedy if their water is harmed.

  2. Property-value buyout at 110% of assessed value. Owners near the project who suffer measured loss can sell at a defined fair price.

  3. Enforceable noise limits. The project must meet defined noise standards, now including light-trespass limits. Extended Oct 2026

  4. Affordable-housing earmarks. A share of community payments is reserved for housing.

Accountability 15 to 20

  1. No NDAs on the public side. Secrecy agreements cannot cover the public side of the deal.

  2. Public developer scorecard before any vote. A public record of the developer's track record, now requiring a cumulative air-quality impact assessment. Extended Oct 2026

  3. Hard milestones with automatic clawbacks. Missed milestones trigger penalties automatically; commitments now include minimum operating duration with exit fees. Extended Oct 2026

  4. Decommissioning bond posted up front. Financial assurance is posted before operations begin, now explicitly covering electronic waste at teardown. Extended Oct 2026

  5. One regional negotiating floor. A common floor across the county or region stops communities from underbidding each other.

  6. Competitive solicitation above the floor. When communities seek projects, they compete openly on terms above the floor.

New terms added October 2026 21 to 30

  1. Labor standards. A project labor agreement, local-hire targets, prevailing wage, and apprenticeships are in the deal, with permanent-job wage floors and benefits, reporting by worker ZIP code and residency, and MWBE and veteran procurement reporting. New

  2. Backup-generator disclosure, emissions limits, and testing windows. The developer discloses generator count, capacity, fuel type, and on-site fuel storage; nitrogen-oxide and particulate-matter emissions are capped; testing has limited hours and windows; and actual operating hours are reported publicly. New

  3. Construction and traffic management. A baseline road-condition study, designated truck routes, bridge limits, and road restoration after construction are required, along with traffic controls and construction-hour restrictions. New

  4. Developer-funded independent review. The developer funds a county-controlled escrow account that pays for the community's own experts: a utility and grid consultant, a water and wastewater engineer, a municipal-finance expert, an environmental consultant, and data-center-specialist counsel. The county chooses its own experts. New

  5. Grid-emergency curtailment, demand response, and interruptible service. The community can require participation in demand response, emergency load reduction, and interruptible service during grid emergencies. Nevada's 2026 framework is a precedent: no cost of serving a data center is borne by any other ratepayer. New

  6. Waste-heat reuse. Heat-recovery options must be evaluated or enabled. New

  7. Responsible siting. Brownfields and industrial lands are preferred over farmland and sensitive lands. New

  8. Creditworthy guarantor and successor liability. A parent-company guarantee or equivalent credit support; obligations bind successors and assigns; the county must approve any assignment or change of control; obligations survive sale of the facility; and replacement security is required if the guarantor's credit deteriorates. New

  9. Take-or-pay and stranded-grid protection. Minimum-load commitments and take-or-pay utility contracts, substantial deposits and security, milestone-based release of reserved capacity, and forfeiture if the project fails to proceed. New

  10. Battery storage and fire/EMS costs. Disclosure of battery chemistry and capacity, a fire-hazard analysis, fire-department preplanning and training, developer-funded specialized equipment, emergency-response planning, and annual public-safety reimbursement, in line with New Jersey's 2026 municipal guidance (Local Finance Notice 2026-13). New

Reading the dollar benchmarks

DeForest is included as a negotiated benchmark even though the proposed QTS project ultimately did not proceed; its draft agreement shows what one community was able to negotiate before final approval.

Term 4's 30-day public comparison is only as honest as the benchmarks it uses. The four comparable deals below were rechecked against October 2026 reporting, but they are three different measures. Read them as a set:

Three different measures, not one.

  • (a) Upfront or lifetime community investment. New York's voluntary framework recommends a community-investment benchmark of at least $1 million per MW of utility demand. It is voluntary guidance — a flexible upfront-or-lifetime investment target negotiated locally, paid upfront or phased over the project's lifetime. It is not a mandatory statewide payment, not an annual per-MW payment, and not necessarily cash to the host community (it can include qualifying infrastructure).
  • (b) Recurring annual tax or PILOT revenue. What the property pays every year in taxes or in lieu of them. West Feliciana's projected figure is about $273,000 per MW of contracted utility demand per year, and that is projected PILOT/lease revenue, not a separate community-benefit payment on top of ordinary taxes.
  • (c) Recurring community payments above taxes. Annual payments negotiated on top of whatever taxes are paid. A separate category entirely.

Do not compare a one-time per-MW figure with an annual per-MW figure. One million dollars per MW once is not the same kind of number as $273,000 per MW every year: they measure different things, and a strong deal can include both.

West Feliciana Parish, Louisiana / Hut 8

Last verified: October 2026Deal comparable
Structure

A PILOT and lease arrangement through the parish Industrial Development Board: developer-donated property is leased back to the developer. This is not full taxation.

Projected public revenue

Parish officials project roughly $90 million per year in PILOT revenue for the project, reported at 330 MW of utility capacity — about $273,000 per MW per year. This is government revenue replacing conventional property taxation, not an additional community-benefit payment.

Water

Hut 8 committed about $16 million for wells and mains to be transferred to the parish, potentially benefiting thousands of households. This is the deal's real community-investment headline.

DeForest, Wisconsin / QTS

Last verified: October 2026Negotiated benchmark
Status

A detailed draft pre-annexation agreement the village negotiated with QTS for a $12 billion, 1,600-acre campus; the project ultimately did not proceed and the village never voted on annexation. Its terms are included here as a negotiated-only benchmark.

A guaranteed assessed value of at least $1.2 billion for 15 years, worth an estimated $12 million per year in property-tax payments to the village, schools, and other taxing bodies — a contractual minimum taxable-value floor.

Last verified: October 2026Benchmark
The benchmark

New York's voluntary framework recommends a community-investment benchmark of at least $1 million per MW of utility demand. It is voluntary guidance — a flexible upfront-or-lifetime investment target negotiated locally, not a mandatory statewide payment, a recurring annual payment, or necessarily cash to the host community. New York's Host Community Investment Framework says it can include state-required grid modernization, can be phased over time, and should consider inflation. Community Investment Framework

Empire State Development, NY (September 2026)
Template / checklistFree downloadTemplate file not verified

Covers: a voluntary template and guidance for municipalities and development agencies negotiating with developers of large data centers (Executive Order No. 62, July 2026; the related moratorium covers projects of 50 megawatts and up). Four pillars: Good Neighbor Commitments (design, noise and light mitigation); labor (prevailing wage, project labor agreements, local hiring, apprenticeships, workforce development); transparency and accountability (economic, environmental, and operational reporting); and targeted investments through a Community Investment Fund (infrastructure, water and sewer, transit, housing, broadband, childcare, schools, blight remediation). New York's voluntary framework recommends a community-investment benchmark of at least $1 million per MW of utility demand. It is voluntary guidance — a flexible upfront-or-lifetime investment target negotiated locally, not a mandatory statewide payment, a recurring annual payment, or necessarily cash to the host community. Related EO-62 work (a grid "beneficiary pays" group, a DEC water report) sits outside the framework itself.

Cost: free download, no login. It is explicitly voluntary, described in its own materials as something communities "may use or disregard."

Verification: the ESD page was read in full, but the linked template files did not open during the research read, so their exact character (fill-in worksheet vs. model contract language) is unconfirmed; the description here combines the ESD page with secondary summaries (dig.watch, Cuddy and Feder, Harris Beach Murtha, and the CIF policy outline PDF).

What the model package took from it: labor standards (term 21, principle 9); light-trespass limits folded into the noise term; the $1M-per-MW benchmark as a standing dollar reference (held as an upfront-or-lifetime measure, distinct from recurring annual revenue; see Reading the dollar benchmarks).

Community Benefits Agreement Template for Data Centers

NAACP Center for Environmental and Climate Justice, drafted with the Columbia Law Sabin Center (2026)
Model contract languageFree downloadRead in full

Covers: jobs (40 to 50% local hiring, prevailing wage, union neutrality, apprenticeships, automation-displacement disclosure); environment (annual standardized public disclosure of energy, water, emissions, and backup fuel; a renewable ratchet to 100% with interim milestones; an independent cumulative impact assessment before permitting and every five years; an adaptive-review trigger); grid and ratepayer transparency (disclose expected system-upgrade costs and who bears them, as a non-cost-shifting principle); equity (equity impact assessments; protections for renters, small businesses, and Black and Indigenous residents); accountability (independent annual audits, a public dashboard, escalating penalties, subsidy clawbacks); a Community Benefit Fund (upfront plus annual inflation-indexed payments, community-majority board); binding language through construction, operation, and decommissioning.

Cost: free download, no login. It is written for community coalitions negotiating private CBAs, not for municipalities, and it leaves dollar figures blank; your office can still borrow its structure and indexation.

Verification: the full template text was read. No decommissioning bond was verified (the decommissioning period is covered; a bond requirement is not confirmed), no NDA-prohibition term, and no regional floor. Companions: "How to Negotiate on CBAs and CAB template" and the Stop Dirty Data Centers campaign page.

What the model package took from it: labor standards (term 21, principle 9); the independent cumulative impact assessment now folded into the pre-vote scorecard (term 16, principle 6).

08Free, login-walled, and paid: access and the NDA question

The most useful finding for a busy official: you do not need to buy anything. The entire public-side landscape is free except for one login-walled guide, and nothing credible was found for sale.

Free downloads

Nearly all

Every publication in section 7 except one is a free download with no paywall and no NDA gate. This includes both full model legal texts (the NAACP CBA template, the Kentucky Resources Council ordinance) and the only government-issued template (New York's Community Investment Framework).

Login-walled

One

The National League of Cities' "Key Questions to Ask When Considering Data Centers in Your Community" (April 2026) requires a login. Its contents are unverified because of that login wall (per the Joyce Foundation / Fresh Coast landscape deck).

Paid

None for sale

No pre-written "negotiation playbook" or term-sheet product is sold to municipalities anywhere the research could find. Paid help is bespoke: law firms and consultants sell their time, not a document.

The paid side

  • Plunkett Cooney (Michigan law firm) is the clearest documented example of a firm selling municipal-side data-center help. Its Data Center Approvals and Regulation practice offers local governments negotiated and drafted development agreements covering community-benefit packages (infrastructure, workforce, revenue sharing), resource-consumption caps, performance guarantees and monitoring, phasing tied to compliance, and decommissioning financial assurances, plus government-relations advocacy and coalition building. It is a paid legal engagement; no public template or playbook is offered.

  • Planning and engineering consultants sell bespoke engagements: for example, McKenna advised the City of Grandville, Michigan on its data-center zoning amendment (per reporting in September 2026). Fresh Coast Climate Solutions and Cascade Energy produced a funder-facing landscape deck (Joyce Foundation, May 2026), marked "proprietary and confidential" but publicly hosted. That deck tracks the publication landscape for funders; it is not a community template.

  • Developer-side firms for contrast: McGuireWoods Consulting, Greenbaum Rowe, Babst Calland, Holland and Hart, and Olshan all run data-center practices that sell to developers, not communities (site selection, incentives, PILOT and redevelopment agreements). If your developer brings counsel from one of these firms, remember who the client is.

The NDA question

No template, guide, or model ordinance was found behind a paywall or an NDA. That said, NDAs are still the central problem in real deals: most data-center deals are negotiated under confidentiality agreements, which is why communities negotiate blind and why this page's model package starts from a no-NDA rule on the public side.

  • WRI cites a township-level NDA ban (Big Rapids Township, Michigan) and NDA-ban bills in 10 states.

  • Public Citizen calls for prohibiting or strictly limiting NDAs binding public officials.

  • Food and Water Watch issued a national call for a ban on all government nondisclosure agreements (September 2026).

  • The model package treats the no-NDA rule as a deal term, distinct from the legislative bans others advocate.

09What no one covers

Two topics were absent from every publication verified, through no one's oversight alone. They are genuinely open research questions for anyone building the next template.

  1. 1.

    Property-tax assessment methodology for data centers. Nobody anywhere covers how a data center should be valued for property-tax purposes. Several publications urge limiting or removing tax breaks, and the model package sets a full-taxation default with a contractual tax floor, but the assessment question itself, how the taxable value is computed, is covered by neither side.

  2. 2.

    Electromagnetic fields (EMF). No template, guide, or ordinance found addresses EMF exposure from data-center facilities. This includes the model package.
