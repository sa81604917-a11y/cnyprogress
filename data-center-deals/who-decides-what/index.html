Onondaga County, New York: a plain-language roles guide

Who decides what

A data center proposal moves through at least six different bodies, and each one controls a different set of decisions. This page explains, in plain language, what each can actually demand or approve and what it cannot. Read it to know where the real leverage sits and which door to knock on.

Shortest version: The town or village with zoning jurisdiction decides whether the proposed use and site plan are allowed. OCIDA decides requested tax incentives. The utility sets proposed service terms, subject to state regulation. County agencies, environmental regulators, and the applicable water and sewer providers decide the permits, capacity, and agreements within their own authority.

01

Onondaga County government

The county sets policy and pays for the outside study of data center impacts, but it cannot approve or stop a specific project in a town. The county legislature and county executive sit in Syracuse.

Can demand or decide

  • Pay for and steer the outside study of data center impacts (announced June 2026; about $500,000), then adopt county policy from it.
  • Appoint the IDA's board, all seven members, by legislature resolution. This is the county's main pull on tax-break decisions.
  • Set countywide expectations: a shared negotiating floor for PILOTs, disclosure rules, and publication of any deals it approves.
  • Fund county infrastructure tied to growth: roads, county sewer work through the Water Environment Protection department.
  • Give county planning staff a review role on some big projects near county lines or county assets under state law. The county's take is a recommendation, not a veto.

Cannot demand or decide

  • Change zoning in any town or village. Zoning is the town's, the village's, or the city's own power.
  • Set electric rates or tell the utility whom to serve. The state utility commission does that.
  • Grant, shrink, or rewrite IDA tax breaks directly. The IDA's board makes those calls; the county's say runs through the board it appoints.
  • Overrule a town planning board on a site plan or a special use permit.

Which door to knock on: county legislators and legislature committee meetings. Watch for the public input process tied to the data center study once dates are announced.

County role and the outside study were reported by regional outlets including NewsChannel 9 in June 2026; the IDA board-appointment rule comes from OCIDA's own financial statements.

02

The industrial development agency (OCIDA)

The IDA decides whether a data center gets tax breaks, how big they are, and what the developer promises in return. It does not decide whether the project gets built.

Can demand or decide

  • Grant or refuse three kinds of tax relief for a project: sales tax exemptions, mortgage recording tax exemptions, and property tax abatements through a PILOT agreement.
  • Decide the PILOT's schedule under its written Uniform Tax Exemption Policy: what the developer pays each year and how it rises or falls over time.
  • Divide PILOT payments among the affected taxing places: the county, the town or village, and the school district.
  • Ask for job creation and investment promises before it grants anything, and set those promises in writing.
  • Write clawback provisions so benefits can be taken back if the project breaks its promises.
  • Choose to follow the state's voluntary Community Investment Framework, including the $1 million per MW benchmark, when it negotiates.

Cannot demand or decide

  • Change or override town zoning. If the town says no to the land use, there is no project to give a tax break to.
  • Set electric or water rates, or order a utility to build new capacity.
  • Approve the project itself. Site plans, environmental review, and state permits live elsewhere.
  • Guarantee that promised jobs go to local people or pay a certain wage. It can set commitments in a deal; delivery is on the developer, with clawbacks as the public's backstop.

Which door to knock on: OCIDA's board, a public body that meets in Syracuse (333 W. Washington St.), and the public record for each project it takes up. Ask to see the draft PILOT schedule and the clawback terms before any vote.

OCIDA was created in 1970 by the county legislature under the state's IDA act; its powers and the PILOT process were described in detail in a 2020 audit by the state comptroller.

03

Towns and villages (zoning and land use)

The town where the land sits decides whether a data center can be built there, and on what conditions. This is the most direct power anyone holds over a specific project. The key players are the town board, the planning board, and the zoning board of appeals.

Can demand or decide

  • Decide whether data centers are an allowed use in a zoning district, a special use needing extra permission, or banned unless the zoning changes.
  • Adopt a data center law before any proposal arrives: where they may go, setbacks from homes, height, screening, noise and light limits, and backup generator testing rules.
  • Attach conditions to site plan or special use approval: traffic and truck routes, construction hours, landscaping, and road repair.
  • Adopt a temporary local moratorium to pause new projects while new rules are written.
  • Lead the state environmental review (SEQR) for a project and require studies on traffic, noise, views, and water before deciding.
  • Require reporting and a decommissioning bond or escrow, paid up front, under local law.

Cannot demand or decide

  • Set or change the price of electricity, or decide who pays for grid upgrades. That lives with the utility regulator.
  • Grant property tax breaks or write PILOTs. Only the IDA does that; the town receives its share of the payments.
  • Overrule state permits. A town cannot waive a DEC permit requirement, and a DEC permit does not waive the town's own zoning. They run in parallel.
  • Regulate the power grid itself: substations, lines, and tariffs sit with the utility and the state.

Which door to knock on: the town board and planning board, at their public hearings on zoning and site plans. For a specific project, this is where resident voices carry the most weight.

Towns get their zoning power from state law (Town Law and the state's local government publications); the toolkit side of this is mapped in model ordinances and county checklists in the sources below.

04

The electric utility (National Grid)

National Grid figures out what a big new user costs the grid and proposes who pays. But the state commission decides the rules.

Can demand or decide

  • Run the interconnection study for a data center: how much power it needs, what must be built (substation, lines, upgrades), and a cost estimate.
  • Sign service contracts with large customers under utility commission approved tariffs.
  • Propose rate structures for huge new users for the commission to approve.
  • Confirm in writing whether existing capacity can carry a project, which towns and the developer can demand before approvals move forward.
  • Refuse a connection that would be unsafe or unreliable, under the rules set for all customers.

Cannot demand or decide

  • Set its own rates. The Public Service Commission approves them.
  • Pick where data centers get built, or change the zoning.
  • Grant tax incentives or write host community agreements.
  • Decide alone who pays for big grid upgrades. If the company cannot make the developer pay through a contract, whether ordinary customers pick up part of the cost is fought out at the commission. The state has ordered its utility staff to study a 'beneficiary pays' approach for large new users.

Which door to knock on: residents cannot negotiate with the utility over a data center's deal. The real door is the state utility commission's public comment process in rate and tariff cases.

National Grid (Niagara Mohawk Power Corporation) is the electric and gas utility for Syracuse and much of upstate New York, acquired by National Grid in 2000.

05

State regulators

The state sets the rules for electricity, air, and water, and right now it is writing new rules specifically for data centers. The key agencies: the Public Service Commission (electric), NYSDEC (environment), and Empire State Development (economic development).

Can demand or decide

  • The Public Service Commission sets electric rates and can approve special tariffs for huge new users ('large-load tariffs': minimum bills, long terms, exit charges). Advocates are pushing for them; nothing is confirmed adopted in National Grid territory as of this page's sources.
  • DEC decides whether the project's backup generators and on-site engines need air permits.
  • DEC permits water withdrawals for any system with the capacity to pull 100,000 gallons a day or more.
  • DEC also permits wastewater discharges to waterways and protects wetlands and stormwater quality.
  • The Governor's July 2026 executive order paused specified state discretionary permits for data centers of 50 MW or more until the Department of Public Service completes a Generic Environmental Impact Statement and findings, with no fixed deadline; whether the pause reaches local approvals is not settled in the public record.
  • Empire State Development published a voluntary Community Investment Framework: a $1 million per MW community-investment benchmark and a negotiation template that towns and IDAs can use or ignore.

Cannot demand or decide

  • Grant local tax breaks or write PILOTs. That is the IDA's call.
  • Rezone land in a town or overrule a local planning board's site plan decision.
  • Approve a project's fit with a local zoning code. State permits and local zoning run on separate tracks.
  • Order a town to host a data center or to change its rules.

Which door to knock on: written comments in utility commission rate and tariff cases; public notice and hearings tied to DEC permits; comments when the state's data center environmental study opens.

The state moratorium and permit hold were reported in September 2026; the Community Investment Framework is published by Empire State Development.

06

Water authorities

Whoever pipes the water in and treats the wastewater decides whether the project gets hooked up, how much capacity it gets, and what it pays.

Can demand or decide

  • OCWA, the Onondaga County Water Authority, supplies drinking water across most of the county outside the City of Syracuse (about 340,000 people across five counties, from Lake Ontario, Otisco Lake, and Skaneateles Lake). It sets rates and decides how it serves big users.
  • The City of Syracuse runs its own water system for the city.
  • The county Water Environment Protection department (WEP) runs the public sewer system in the Consolidated Sanitary District, reviews every new sewer service request for available capacity, issues industrial discharge permits, and monitors what big users send to the plants.
  • Both systems can put the cost of extensions and upgrades the project forces onto the developer through their service terms. This is where 'costs the project causes are not community benefits' gets enforced for water.
  • Water is not a free good here. In December 2025 a broken 42-inch OCWA main put 27,000 accounts under conservation orders. A heavy new user is a real ask of that system.

Cannot demand or decide

  • Override state water, health, or environmental permits.
  • Set electric rates or grant tax breaks.
  • Decide zoning; the water body can't block a project outside its own hookup and capacity decisions.

Which door to knock on: OCWA for water service and capacity questions; WEP for sewer discharge permits and capacity review.

OCWA's service area comes from its public records; WEP's capacity and discharge-permit roles are stated on the county's own WEP pages.

Where the leverage sits

  • Two gates matter most. The town's land-use approval and the IDA's tax-break decision shape any deal more than anything else. Silence the tax break and the project may still get built; change the zoning and it often cannot get built at all.
  • Write the rules before the proposal arrives. A town that adopts data center setbacks, noise limits, and a special use requirement before a developer shows up holds the cards. So does an IDA that insists on full PILOT schedules and real clawbacks as the price of subsidies.
  • Money lives at the IDA table. PILOTs, host payments, investment commitments, and clawbacks are negotiated with the IDA, not the town planning board.
  • Power costs are the state's arena. Electric rates, large-load tariffs, and who pays for grid upgrades are decided by the utility commission, not by towns or the county. Residents have the least direct say here, and the public comment process is their route in.
  • The county sets the table. Appointing the IDA board, running the study, and adopting a countywide floor that towns can take up: that is what county-level power looks like.

Open questions

Public facts that are not settled, so this page will not pretend they are:

  • What the state's one-year environmental study and its grid funding work will conclude, and what rules they will produce.
  • Whether the utility commission adopts large-load tariffs for National Grid customers. Campaigned for, not confirmed.
  • Which towns and villages will actually see proposals, and what each one's zoning code currently requires. This page did not check every town's code; verify for any specific project.
  • What OCIDA's current written tax exemption policy says term by term, and how it might change for a data center deal. Ask the agency for the text.
  • Purchasing and merger rules change often. The utility facts here were checked in October 2026 and can change with new orders.

Glossary

MW (megawatt)
A unit of electrical power demand. A data center of 50 MW uses roughly what tens of thousands of homes use at peak times. The state's one-year permit review applies at 50 MW or more.
PILOT
Payment in lieu of taxes. When an IDA holds title to a project, the property leaves the tax rolls; the PILOT agreement sets what the developer pays instead, and how the money is split among the county, town or village, and school district.
IDA / OCIDA
Industrial development agency. In New York these are county or local public benefit corporations that can grant tax incentives. OCIDA is the one for Onondaga County.
UTEP
Uniform Tax Exemption Policy. The written policy each IDA must keep, describing the tax breaks it offers and the rules and recapture terms attached to them.
Clawback
A deal term that takes tax breaks or cash back when the developer breaks its promises on jobs, investment, or other commitments.
SEQR
The State Environmental Quality Review Act. New York's review process for the environmental impacts of agency decisions, including project approvals.
Large-load tariff
A special utility rate structure for very large new electricity users, often with minimum bills, long contract terms, and exit charges so the public does not end up paying for infrastructure a big user stops needing.
Beneficiary pays
The principle that the new large user, not ordinary customers, should pay for the grid upgrades its load triggers. The state has ordered its utility staff to study this for data centers.
Host community agreement / CBA
A contract through which the developer funds local projects or services (roads, schools, emergency response). 'CBA' means community benefits agreement; host community agreement is the common New York label.
Home rule
In this context, the long standing New York rule that towns, villages, and cities control their own zoning and land use unless the state clearly takes that power back.
