How Towns Protect Themselves: Know the Developer’s Playbook

October 2026

In October 2026, five newsrooms across Oregon, Pennsylvania, Michigan, New Jersey, and Alabama pooled their reporting on how data center developers win land from reluctant towns. Per Planetizen’s summary of the Oregonian’s investigation, the moves include lawsuits against towns that decline to sell, $10,000 offers to every household in Hazle, Pennsylvania, and NDAs that keep local officials from telling residents what is planned.

These are not data-center-only tactics. Any large industrial developer — a chip fab, a distribution hub, a power plant — can use the same playbook. Communities that recognize the moves in advance negotiate from strength. Communities that don’t, don’t.

The developer’s playbook

1. Secrecy first. Developers ask elected officials and economic-development staff to sign NDAs before disclosing what is planned, so residents learn about the project late — often after key decisions are effectively made. Confidentiality clauses can also hide a facility’s true water and energy footprint inside private agreements.

2. Divide and conquer. Neighboring towns are played against one another for the same project, so each undercuts the next on taxes, water, and concessions. The developer’s leverage depends on towns negotiating alone.

3. Sue when told no. When Saline Township, Michigan voted 4–1 to deny the $16 billion OpenAI–Oracle Stargate project, the developers sued under an exclusionary-zoning theory, settled, and broke ground anyway — extracting only about $14 million in community benefits from a $16 billion project. The lawsuit is the business model: make “no” more expensive than “yes.”

4. Inflate the benefits. Research from Syracuse University’s Austin Zwick, published in the Journal of the American Planning Association, finds that data centers and fracking impose nearly identical local burdens — water use, road damage, high construction employment followed by very few permanent jobs — and that industry-funded studies consistently overstate the job numbers for both.

5. Rush the clock. States fast-track approvals, and utilities file rate contracts before public comment periods close. Speed is a tactic: a town that has not written its terms before the proposal arrives will improvise them under deadline, badly.

The community’s defenses

Every one of these moves has a tested counter. None of them requires banning anything.

1. Regulate, don’t ban. Zwick’s central finding: outright bans are mostly overturned by states — it is already happening with data centers in West Virginia — while targeted local rules hold up. Setback requirements, performance bonds, noise and light standards, conditional-use review, and enforceable community benefits agreements have produced far better outcomes. Loudoun County, Virginia, which replaced automatic by-right approval with public hearings and conditional permits, is the model to copy.

2. Buy time with a moratorium. At least 19 Michigan townships have enacted data center moratoriums; Prince George’s County, Maryland passed a two-year pause; Merrillville, Indiana imposed a one-year halt to study real-world impacts next door before deciding. A pause is not a ban. It is time to write the rules.

3. Ban NDAs for public officials. Portland, Oregon passed a resolution discouraging NDAs with data center developers and requiring notification when a company seeks to build or expand a facility drawing 20 megawatts or more. Big Rapids Township, Michigan went further with an ordinance prohibiting elected officials, staff, and development authorities from signing blanket NDAs. Legislation to ban them has been introduced in 10 states. Narrow, temporary protections for genuine trade secrets are fine; blanket secrecy about public-resource deals is not.

4. Lock in enforceable community benefits agreements — with exit fees. Lancaster, Pennsylvania capped a data center’s daily water use and restricted generator testing to daytime hours. Cedar Rapids, Iowa requires annual contributions to a city betterment fund. And as the Brookings Institution notes, agreements should include exit fees for early closure, so taxpayers are not stranded if the project folds — plus terms for repurposing the site.

5. Cap water and power, and make the developer pay for upgrades. Kingsbury County, South Dakota requires data centers to hit a maximum water-usage-effectiveness standard and source at least half of cooling water from nonpotable supplies. California’s 2026 package makes data centers pay their fair share of grid-upgrade costs so residents don’t subsidize them, and requires water-use disclosure under penalty of perjury. Closer to home, Henrietta, New York adopted a local law setting thresholds for power, water, and noise — and requiring applicants to propose mitigations and community benefits.

6. Fund your own experts. Require the developer to pay into a municipal fund so the town can hire independent legal, hydrology, and grid experts during negotiations. Otherwise it is their engineers against your town board, and that is not a negotiation.

7. Negotiate regionally, not town by town. A shared regional framework for land use, infrastructure, and community benefits keeps neighboring municipalities from undercutting each other. The developer’s divide-and-conquer strategy only works if towns let it.

8. Document early. Residents near data centers have viable legal claims — nuisance, property-value diminishment, environmental-review failures, water rights, due process — but those claims require documentation that must begin before, not after, construction starts. Start the paper trail the day the rumor arrives.

What this means for Central New York

Micron’s Clay megafab was negotiated largely behind closed doors, which is exactly the secrecy-first move described above. The community benefits agreement push is the corrective: published terms, negotiated in public, before the next developer arrives. New York’s Host Community Investment Framework — roughly $1 million per megawatt as a starting benchmark — is a floor, not a ceiling.
