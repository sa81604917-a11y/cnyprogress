Case study: Indianapolis–Metrobloks

A $3.4 billion data center, $183 million in tax savings, and a neighborhood still deciding whether to fight the project or take the money. A live example of what to fix before the vote.

Published October 13, 2026. The Metropolitan Development Commission votes on the tax package on October 21, 2026, at 1 p.m. at the City-County Building. Figures below are from reporting available before the vote and may change.

The deal in numbers

Metrobloks data center, 2505 N. Sherman Drive, Martindale Brightwood
Developer Metrobloks (Los Angeles-based); site is the former Sherman Drive-in Theater
Promised investment About $3.4 billion, down from an earlier $7 billion estimate (per the Indy Economic Development Inc.)
Total tax savings More than $183 million in reported tax savings for Metrobloks and future tenants — roughly an 87% overall tax savings (the itemized components disclosed publicly sum to about $155 million; see breakdown)
Savings breakdown $12.9M (15-year property tax abatement) + $14.3M (10-year, 90% personal property tax abatement) + more than $128M for future tenants’ equipment (personal property tax) — about $155 million itemized; public reporting puts the package total above $183 million
Permanent jobs 35 full-time — about $777,000 in Metrobloks-attributable incentives per job ($12.9M + $14.3M ÷ 35); about $5.2 million per job if the entire $183M package, including $128M+ for future tenants’ equipment, is charged against Metrobloks’ 35 jobs
Community payment At least $22.5 million, potentially up to $32.5 million, for street improvements, parks, alleys, and a community grant fund — about 12% of the tax savings, versus the city’s 5% minimum guideline
Status Rezoning approved April 2026; the company holds permission to break ground any day. Tax package negotiated by the Indy Economic Development Inc. (created early 2025).

Why it looks unfavorable

  • The incentive fails the but-for test. Tax incentives are supposed to change behavior — to cause investment that would not otherwise happen. Metrobloks already has its rezoning and can break ground any day. If the project would proceed without the package, the $183 million is a transfer, not an incentive — which is exactly what an independent but-for finding is meant to test.
  • About $777,000 per Metrobloks job. Thirty-five permanent jobs against $27.2 million in company-specific abatements — and $5.2 million per job if the whole $183 million package is charged against those 35 jobs. No economic-development standard justifies either ratio, and the jobs are expected to go to highly skilled workers with advanced degrees — in a neighborhood where only about 12% of residents have education beyond high school (2024 census projections).
  • The investment already shrank; the incentive did not. The project went from a $7 billion estimate to $3.4 billion. Headline investment figures move; abatements should be tied to verified spend, with clawbacks if the numbers do not materialize.
  • The community money has no plumbing. No administrator has been chosen, no allocation plan exists, and the first dollars are at least a year out. The grant would likely go to a foundation and be distributed over more than a decade. A $22.5 million promise with no governance is not $22.5 million yet.
  • The negotiation happened in private; the community met the terms two weeks before the vote. The package was negotiated between the company, IEDI, and the area’s councilor. The October 7 public meeting — meant as outreach — became a shouting match, with organizers calling it box-checking and residents distrusting both the city agency and each other.

What is genuinely better than the old pattern

  • A community-benefit floor exists and is being exceeded. Indianapolis guidelines require at least 5% of the abatement award to go back into the nearby community. Metrobloks is at about 12% — more than double the floor. The floor is working as a minimum; the question is whether the minimum is aimed at the right thing.
  • A dedicated negotiator exists. IEDI was created in early 2025 to negotiate incentive packages professionally rather than ad hoc. The institution is an improvement even when its current product is not.
  • There is a public vote at all. The Metropolitan Development Commission votes in public on October 21. That is the leverage point — and the reason the analysis below is framed as fixes before the vote, not complaints after it.

Six things Indianapolis should fix before October 21

  1. Require an independent but-for finding. Before approving, the Commission should require a written finding — not from the developer — that the project would not proceed without the package. On the current public record (rezoning approved, shovel-ready), an independent analyst would struggle to make that finding — though only the company knows its true walk-away economics.
  2. Cap incentives per job. A per-job ceiling (for example, a fixed dollar maximum per permanent full-time job) would have flagged this deal instantly. $777,000 per job — or $5.2 million on the whole-package math — should never clear a vote without extraordinary justification.
  3. Tie the abatement to verified investment, with clawbacks. The project already fell from $7 billion to $3.4 billion. Write the abatement against audited capital spend and job counts, with automatic reduction and repayment if targets are missed.
  4. Lock community-fund governance before the vote. Name the administrator, publish the allocation criteria, set the disbursement timeline — and put the community representatives who will oversee it in the agreement, not in a promise to figure it out later.
  5. Disclose water, power, and grid costs — and assign them. The lasting local costs of a data center are utility strain and grid upgrades. Require the developer to publish projected demand and state in writing who pays for the infrastructure it triggers. Costs the project causes are not community benefits.
  6. Negotiate in public from the start. Bring community representatives into the negotiation while terms are still movable, not to a presentation two weeks before the vote. The distrust in Martindale Brightwood is a predictable product of the process, not a failure of the residents.

For comparison within the same city: Sabey Data Center Properties’ $4.3 billion Decatur Township project carries a $240 million tax-savings deal with at least $28.5 million committed to road improvements and a new aquatic center — roughly the same ~12% community share. Twelve percent is the going rate in Indianapolis. The question for the Commission is whether the going rate is good enough, and whether any rate is justified when the but-for test fails.
