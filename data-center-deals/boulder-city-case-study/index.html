Case study: Boulder City and the federal-land loophole

Residents defeated a data center on private land. The developer moved to adjacent federal land, and the Bureau of Land Management signed off. When local opposition wins, the project can simply leave local jurisdiction behind.

Published October 13, 2026. Figures below are from reporting available at publication and may change.

What happened

Townsite data center, near Boulder City, Nevada
Town Boulder City, Nevada, less than an hour from the Las Vegas Strip
Summer 2026 Residents, including Janelle Sorenson, successfully opposed a data center proposed on private land. The developer withdrew its application.
Then The developer sought adjacent federally owned land instead — and the U.S. Bureau of Land Management signed off. Residents learned of the reroute after the fact: “we were just really kept in the dark.”
First of its kind Townsite was the first data center in the country approved on federal public land. Construction is stalled after a judge ruled the government could not reuse an old environmental review from when the project was intended to be a solar farm.
Scale of what is coming More than a dozen similar federal-land projects are in the planning stages. Data centers can run as large as 70 football fields. The Trump administration is advancing them through the FAST-41 permitting program; the permitting council has called federal-land data centers “really ideal candidates.”
Why Nevada 80% of Nevada’s land is federally owned — the highest share of any state. The issue has become dominant in the state’s toss-up governor’s race (Cook Political Report).

The loophole, stated plainly

This toolkit argues that permanent local bans are self-defeating: a ban in one town moves the project to the next town over, which then negotiates without your terms. (Temporary moratoria — pauses to write the rules — are a different tool, and this toolkit endorses them.) Boulder City inverts the argument. The residents did everything right — they organized, they won — and the project moved beyond any town’s jurisdiction at all.

The mechanism:

  1. Community defeats a project on private land through local process.
  2. Developer re-sites on adjacent federal land.
  3. Federal approval bypasses every local protection the community just fought for — zoning, water terms, community benefits, even the right to be heard.

The result is worse than the next-town problem. The next town can at least negotiate. Federal land leaves the host community with no seat at the table and no share of the value — all of the burdens (water, power strain, landscape) with none of the leverage.

What federal permitting should require

The principles in this toolkit’s term sheet were written for local officials, but the federal-land loophole means they need a federal counterpart. At a minimum:

  1. Fresh environmental review, every time. The Townsite stall happened because a judge rejected a recycled review from the site’s solar-farm era. That should be the rule, not a lawsuit outcome — no repurposed reviews for projects of this scale.
  2. Developer-paid infrastructure and power. The same standard both Nevada gubernatorial candidates have now converged on: data centers pay their own electricity and grid costs so ratepayers’ bills do not rise.
  3. Water disclosure and limits. Arid-state data centers must disclose projected consumption and live within enforceable caps — not evaporative cooling on the public’s aquifer.
  4. Host-community standing. Adjacent communities should have formal standing in the federal review — notice, comment with response, and a defined share of any community investment — so “kept in the dark” is not the default outcome.
  5. No tax-advantaged double dip. Projects that bypass local taxation by siting federally should not also collect federal tax advantages without equivalent public return.

A note on the Nevada race

Both candidates have landed in the same place on the core economics: developers should pay their own way. Governor Lombardo signed a September 2026 executive order requiring data center developers to cover project costs so they are not passed to ratepayers; challenger Aaron Ford has pledged to pause new data center tax breaks, ban water-intensive evaporative cooling, and require developers to bring their own water and pay their own power. This case study takes no position on the race. The point is structural: whichever candidate wins, the federal-land loophole will still be there unless federal permitting itself sets terms.

Why this matters beyond Nevada

Nevada is the extreme case because 80% of its land is federal, but the mechanism travels. Any state with large federal holdings — and a federal administration actively steering data centers onto public land through FAST-41 — can see the same reroute. Local negotiation toolkits remain necessary, but they are no longer sufficient. The next layer of the work is federal permitting standards that carry the same principles: disclose everything, developers pay their costs, communities have standing, and nothing built on a recycled review.
