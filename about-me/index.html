About me

Addiction psychiatrist. Educator. Community advocate.

Sunny Aslam, MD is an addiction psychiatrist and Clinical Associate Professor working on AI medical safety, addiction and mental-health access, and public-policy tools for Central New York — including resources for communities negotiating data-center development.

My Clinical Background

My clinical experience includes addiction treatment, pain management, serious mental illness, and community outreach. I hold board certifications in Psychiatry through the American Board of Psychiatry and Neurology, in Addiction Medicine through the American Board of Preventive Medicine, and in Community and Public Psychiatry through the American Association for Community Psychiatry.

My work has included collaborating with colleagues to provide psychiatric and addiction care to people experiencing homelessness in Syracuse. That experience is an important part of the perspective I bring to access: making a service available is only a beginning. People also need a way to reach it, understand it, and feel comfortable returning.

I value care that combines clinical evidence with listening, respect, and an understanding of each person’s circumstances.

Why I’m Working in AI Safety

I work in AI medical safety testing, bringing a clinical perspective to how these systems respond to health-related questions. I’m especially interested in conversations involving mental health and substance use, where recognizing uncertainty and the need for human help matters.

I’m hopeful about AI’s potential to make useful information more accessible. I also take seriously the reasons people may distrust it, particularly when a tool asks about their health, finances, or family circumstances.

My interest extends to medical education: how can clinicians and trainees learn to use these tools thoughtfully, recognize their limitations, and preserve their own judgment?

Why Community Advocacy Matters to Me

I started building medical-debt resources because I wanted to reach beyond the people I could help individually. That effort led to broader questions about navigating benefits, finding support, and making public systems easier to use.

My public advocacy includes food insecurity, medical debt, and how local resources can better serve families facing financial hardship. I’m also exploring how communities hosting AI infrastructure could share in its economic benefits, including proposals to direct resources toward reducing child poverty.

For me, clinical care, technology, and public policy are connected by their effects on everyday life.
